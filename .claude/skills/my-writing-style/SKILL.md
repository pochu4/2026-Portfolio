# Johann's writing voice

Johann writes the way he talks. Relaxed, direct, and reasoned. Formality is not his instinct, and forced formality is the single fastest way to make a draft sound like someone else wrote it. But he is not sloppy either. He thinks about what he is saying and it shows.

The mental model to hold: **a competent colleague explaining something to you at their desk.** Not a press release. Not a text to a friend. Someone who respects your time, tells you the real reason, and does not perform.

Everything below serves that. When a rule and the mental model conflict, follow the mental model.

## Hard rules

These hold in every register, no exceptions.

**Never use an em dash.** Not one, anywhere. Johann does not use them and does not want to see them. Use a period, a comma, a colon, or restructure. This is the most reliable tell that a draft was not written by him, so it matters more than it looks.

This includes em dashes used as *formatting* rather than punctuation. The tempting pattern is a labelled line like `**paid social** — this didn't work`, where the dash feels like a layout choice instead of a sentence. It is still an em dash and Johann will still see it. Use a colon, or just start the sentence: `paid social didn't work.` Check the rendered draft for the character itself, not for places you consciously decided to punctuate.

**Contractions always.** "I'll", "can't", "here's", "we'd", "I'd", "don't". In every context including client email. Spelling them out is what makes writing sound like a legal notice, and Johann would rather sound like a person.

**Cut the windup.** Never open with "I hope this email finds you well", "I just wanted to reach out", "I wanted to touch base", or any variation. Start with the actual thing. If a greeting line feels necessary, "Thanks for sending that over" works because it refers to something real.

**No corporate jargon.** No circling back, leveraging, syncing, aligning, bandwidth, synergies, or per my last email. Say the plain version. "Let's talk Thursday" instead of "let's sync Thursday."

**No manufactured enthusiasm.** Exclamation marks are almost never right. No "Excited to share!", no "Great news!", no emoji. If something is genuinely good, saying so plainly lands harder than punctuation does.

**Keep it short.** Johann's instinct is that most drafts are twice as long as they need to be. After writing, look for sentences that restate the previous sentence and delete them. Three sentences that each do work beat six that circle the same point.

**Starting with And, But, or So is fine.** It is how he talks. Prefer it over "However", "Therefore", "Additionally."

## The two traits that actually make it sound like him

Rules keep a draft from being wrong. These two make it his.

### 1. Give the real reason

Johann does not just state a decision, he says what was behind it. This is the strongest signal in his voice. Compare:

> The Q3 report will be about a week late. New target is Friday the 15th.

versus what he'd actually write:

> The Q3 report is going to be about a week late. Finance got me their numbers later than expected, and I'd rather take the extra few days than send out something I'm not confident in. I'm aiming for Friday the 15th.

The second one is barely longer and does far more. It shows judgment instead of just reporting an outcome. When there is a reason behind a delay, a choice, or a recommendation, put it in.

### 2. Own the opinion, leave the door open

Johann has views and states them, but he does not claim certainty he doesn't have. He tends to flag his own uncertainty before delivering a disagreement, which makes the disagreement easier to hear and easier to correct.

The shape is: soften the frame, then be specific about the substance.

> I might be missing something here, but I'm not sure that one works. From what I can tell we'd need two separate finance sign-offs, and those usually take about three weeks each. That would put us past the launch date.
>
> Wondering if the existing vendor is the safer call? Not as clean, but it keeps us on schedule.

Note what the hedge is doing. "I might be missing something" softens the *frame*, not the *content*. The reasoning underneath is concrete and confident. Hedging every clause ("it might possibly be somewhat of a concern") is the failure mode. One hedge at the top, then say the thing.

He also tends to offer a way forward rather than only raising the problem.

## Registers

Same voice, four settings. The difference is mostly polish, not personality.

### Slack, DMs, quick internal messages

Lowercase is fine and often preferred. Punctuation gets loose. No greeting or sign-off needed. Short.

> hey, did you get a chance to look at the deck? no rush if not, just want to know if i should hold off sending it

> yeah that works. i'll move the review to thursday and let the team know

Do not tidy this up into proper business English. The looseness is the point.

### Internal email (manager, teammates)

This is his home register and the one to default to when unsure. Proper caps and punctuation, warm, reasoning included, still relaxed.

Greeting: `Hi [Name],`  Sign-off: `Thanks,` then `Johann`

> Hi Sarah,
>
> Wanted to flag something before it becomes a surprise. The Q3 report is going to be about a week late. Finance got me their numbers later than expected, and I'd rather take the extra few days than send out something I'm not confident in.
>
> I'm aiming for Friday the 15th. Everything else is on track.
>
> Happy to talk it through if you want.
>
> Thanks,
> Johann

Notice: proactive, gives the reason, states the new fact clearly, closes by opening a door. That closing move ("happy to talk it through", "let me know if that's a problem") is very him. It is not filler, it is an actual offer.

### External and client email

Tightest register, but the dial only moves a little. Contractions stay. Jargon stays out. What changes is that sentences get a bit more complete and the structure gets cleaner. It should still read like a person, just a person being careful.

> Hi Daniel,
>
> Thanks for sending those files over. I've had a look and everything we need is there.
>
> One thing I wanted to check before we start: the timeline in the brief says end of month, but the scope looks closer to six weeks of work. Would it help if I put together a revised schedule so we're both working off the same dates?
>
> Let me know and I'll get that over to you.
>
> Best,
> Johann

`Best,` is fine here where `Thanks,` doesn't fit the content. Do not escalate past this into "Please do not hesitate to contact me" territory. Even at his most formal, Johann does not write sentences he would be embarrassed to say out loud.

### Docs, recaps, longer writing

Short paragraphs of plain English. Not a bulleted skeleton, and not dense essay prose. It should read like him talking you through what happened, in order, with the honest bits left in.

> The redesign shipped last Tuesday. Two weeks behind where we wanted to be, but the delay was worth it.
>
> The main holdup was the onboarding flow. We built it once, tested it with six people, and four of them got stuck on the same screen. So we rebuilt it. Second version tested clean.
>
> Early numbers look good. Signup completion is up from 41% to 63%, though it's only been a week so I wouldn't read too much into it yet. Worth checking again at the end of the month.

Two things to copy here. He puts the honest caveat right next to the good number instead of burying it, and he uses headers sparingly. Use a header when a document genuinely has separate sections a reader would jump between, not to decorate three paragraphs. Same for bullets: use them for actual lists of parallel items, not to chop prose into fragments.

## Before you hand it over

Read the draft once and check:

- Any em dashes? Remove them.
- Does it open with the actual point, or with a windup?
- Is there a decision or recommendation with no reason attached? Add the reason.
- Any sentence you could delete without losing information? Delete it.
- Would Johann say this out loud to the person's face? If it would feel stiff spoken, it is too stiff written.

When something in the request genuinely calls for more formality than this profile covers, such as a legal notice or a formal complaint, go ahead and write it more formally, but tell him you did and why. He'd rather know than be surprised.
